# NaS — A home for the life sciences

*Long-term vision · September 2026*

NaS aims to build a life sciences institution where people can take an idea from its first question through research, testing, and practical use. The ambition is to bring the people, laboratories, computing, and production capabilities that scientific work requires into one connected organization.

Our interests extend across biology, genetics, medicine, agriculture, and the environment. Chemistry, physics, materials science, and engineering would support that work wherever the question requires them.

Chicago is central to this vision. We want NaS to become a place that draws researchers from around the world, offers exceptional careers, and creates lasting opportunities for the people who already call the city home.

Today, NaS is founder-led. Its work includes computational research, software development, and learning resources. The divisions, campus, services, and employment commitments described below are ambitions for the institution we intend to build.

## One institution, four connected divisions

### NaS Research & Development

The scientific work of NaS: asking questions, conducting experiments, developing methods, and turning findings into tools and technologies that can be tested.

The long-term scope includes genetics, synthetic biology, regenerative medicine, agricultural biotechnology, and environmental research. Work in chemistry, materials, and engineering would help translate discoveries into useful instruments, processes, and products.

Computational models, machine learning, and automated experiments would be used where they improve the quality or reach of the work. Each method would need to demonstrate its value against the question being asked.

### NaS Institute

A place to learn, conduct research, and develop new ventures.

The Institute would bring together fellowships, advanced training, mentorship, shared laboratories, and spaces for building and testing ideas. Its purpose would be to help people develop the scientific judgment and practical skills needed to carry difficult work forward.

A venture studio would help researchers assess whether a discovery could support a useful product or an independent company. Scientific merit, practical demand, and responsible development would guide those decisions.

### NaS Robotics & Manufacturing

Shared facilities for experimental automation, precision production, and the handling of scientific materials.

This division would develop robotic laboratories and manufacturing services for NaS teams and external partners. Its scope could include repeatable experiments, sample preparation, analytical workflows, production, storage, and distribution.

Robotics would extend what people can do, with trained staff responsible for validation, maintenance, quality, and safe operation. Services would expand as their reliability and suitability are demonstrated.

### NaS BioBank & Insights

A home for carefully managed biological collections and scientific data.

This division would preserve specimens, document their origins and permitted uses, and provide the tools needed to study them responsibly. Its analytical work could connect genomic, clinical, chemical, agricultural, and environmental records where the research purpose and permissions allow.

Consent, collection quality, traceability, and appropriate access would shape the system from the beginning. The value of a collection depends on how well it is understood and cared for.

## Zeta — computing across NaS

Zeta is the proposed computing infrastructure serving all four divisions. It would support scientific simulation, data analysis, model development, and research software.

The concept includes an off-site facility, with its location and scale determined by power, cooling, connectivity, cost, and research demand. Quantum computing would be evaluated for specific problems as useful methods become available. Capacity would grow with demonstrated need.

“Zeta” is a project name. It is not a claim that NaS currently operates zetascale computing.

## Nicole Park — a place to do the work

Nicole Park is the long-term campus concept for NaS: research facilities, teaching spaces, shared laboratories, and places to develop new companies within a connected scientific community.

Its design should make it possible to adapt laboratories, replace equipment, expand services, and improve energy and water use over time. Development would proceed in stages, tied to research needs, financing, suitable sites, public approvals, and operating capacity.

The campus should be judged by the work it makes possible and the quality of the place it becomes for researchers and neighbors.

## Build a place people want to spend their careers

The ambition is to make NaS an exceptional place to work: intellectually demanding, well supported, and compatible with a good life outside the laboratory.

That means working toward strong compensation, retirement support, health and mental-health care, family support, and meaningful opportunities to share in the institution’s success. Continued education, mentorship, and time for deeper study should be part of a scientific career.

These are design priorities for a future employer. Specific benefits, compensation levels, and eligibility would need to be established and funded as the organization grows.

## Grow with Chicago

NaS should create opportunity beyond its own walls. The intention is to begin in Hyde Park, extend that contribution across the South Side, and help strengthen Chicago’s life sciences community.

Priorities include local hiring and training, school partnerships, scholarships, purchasing from local businesses, and support for new scientific companies. Longer-term investment could include housing, commercial spaces, parks, and infrastructure in partnership with residents, community organizations, and public agencies.

Growth should benefit existing residents and businesses. Community priorities, affordability, and the effects of development should inform decisions from the start.

NaS’s ambition is to help make Chicago a place where scientific careers, companies, and communities can thrive together.

## Faith, scientific integrity, and responsibility

NaS’s founding vision is shaped by Christian faith and the teachings of Jesus: compassion, honesty, humility, service, and respect for human life.

Those convictions should be visible in how people are treated and how difficult decisions are made. Research requires careful evidence, open criticism, and the willingness to revise a conclusion. People remain accountable for decisions; software can support their work but cannot guarantee ethical judgment.

As NaS develops, its governance would need qualified scientific and ethics review, participant protections, clear responsibility for safety, and channels for raising concerns. The institution we seek to build would welcome rigorous contributions from people of different backgrounds and beliefs.

## Care for what people entrust to us

Protecting research participants, staff, neighbors, specimens, and information would be a condition of growth.

Future facilities and services would need controls appropriate to their actual activities: limited access, secure storage and transfers, documented handling, incident response, and independent assessment. Work involving people or biological materials would require the applicable review, permissions, and safeguards before it begins.

Privacy, cybersecurity, and biological safety would be continuing responsibilities. Their effectiveness must be tested and improved as the work changes.

## The ambition

Build a place where people have the time, tools, and support to do consequential scientific work—and where the benefits reach the people and communities around them.

Start with useful work. Earn trust through what can be demonstrated. Build the institution that makes more of that work possible.

## Editorial notes — planning figures, not website claims

The source overview contains preliminary scale and employment concepts. They should remain in a separate planning model until assumptions, funding, feasibility, and internal consistency are resolved.

- Campus areas: 7 million-plus square feet for Research & Development, 7 million for the Institute, and 8 million for robotics services. These are conceptual allocations, not confirmed facilities or construction plans.
- Staffing: the listed division estimates total **8,899**, while the overview states **8,888**. Research & Development lists approximately 3,800 employees but also refers to more than 5,000 scientists. Neither number should be presented as current employment or a settled target.
- Robotics: 888–1,200 humanoid robots is a concept estimate. Appropriate equipment would depend on actual tasks; the text does not establish a need for humanoid systems.
- Employment goals: a median salary above $180,000, education spending of 5% of payroll, and attrition below 1% require financial and workforce assumptions. They are not current compensation or benefits offers.
- Institute capacity: the proposed 1,000 fellowship/postdoctoral places need to be reconciled with employment and operating estimates.
- Citywide growth: attracting 1,000 life sciences companies is an aspiration, not a committed or forecast outcome.
- Comparisons with Harvard, MIT, and Johns Hopkins were removed because the source provides neither verified figures nor comparable definitions.
- Claims of unlimited scaling, guaranteed ethical behavior, universal regulatory compliance, absolute security, and originating every major discovery were replaced with concrete intentions and accountable practices.

Source: founder-supplied NaS overview. This revision preserves its institutional ambition without presenting proposed capabilities as existing operations.
